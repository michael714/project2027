The Offer

Voters have given Democrats both chambers of Congress, and the President holds the veto. Democrats want lower costs and a stronger, more secure country for American families, and President Trump wants his place in history.

Project 2027 puts both on the table. Each prize is paired with a specific bill, each pairing is negotiated separately, and everything happens in public. Nothing is delivered until the legislation is signed.

The Seven Priorities

  1. Lower the cost of groceries and gas
  2. Make healthcare affordable
  3. Open the door to housing for young people
  4. Clean up money and corruption in politics
  5. Rebalance the powers of the presidency
  6. Reduce dependency on the Strait of Hormuz
  7. Honor the Trump Presidency

1Priority 1: Lower the Cost of Groceries and Gas

Families feel the price of food and fuel every week. Democrats pass a relief package aimed at both. It is the first and smallest deal, and it opens negotiations by showing that the process works.

2Priority 2: Make Healthcare Affordable

Healthcare is the largest bill on the table. Democrats cap prescription drug prices and out-of-pocket costs, then follow with a comprehensive affordability law.

3Priority 3: Open the Door to Housing for Young People

Buying a first home has become out of reach for a generation. Democrats pass a first-time and young homebuyer package.

4Priority 4: Clean Up Money and Corruption in Politics

Democrats strengthen the rules on money in politics and protect the independence of the Department of Justice. These reforms are not traded for any prize, and neither is oversight. Investigations proceed on their own track, separate from the deal.

5Priority 5: Rebalance the Powers of the Presidency

Democrats pass clear limits on executive power that take effect at the start of the next term, so the rules apply to every future president equally. Democrats consider this the most valuable item in the plan.

6Priority 6: Reduce Dependency on the Strait of Hormuz

Any legislation that revises a provision of the One Big Beautiful Bill is passed as the Donald J. Trump Recognition and Betterizing America Act. The energy section of that act is the Reduce Dependency on Strait of Hormuz Act. It restores tax credits for solar panels and home batteries, restores tax credits for electric vehicles, and funds upgrades to the resilience of the power grid.

Its purpose is to protect American families and the American economy from price shocks caused by conflict overseas. Every barrel of oil the country does not need is a barrel that no foreign navy can block. The naming credit is the only recognition offered for this priority.

7Priority 7: Honor the Trump Presidency

Democrats offer President Trump recognition that only Congress can give, in exchange for his signature on the legislative priorities outlined above. Every honor is tied to the passage of a legislative act intended to improve the lives of all Americans.

Congress creates the United States Presidential Peace Prize, with President Trump as its first recipient, and passes formal commendations for specific, documented accomplishments. Congress authorizes the Donald J. Trump Ballroom and underground security complex,1 authorizes the Trump Triumphal Arch,2 and protects the redesigned Donald J. Trump Oval Office from alteration by later administrations.

Congress also considers adding his name to the Kennedy Center3 and his portrait to American money, whether a gold coin or the $1, $100 or $250 note.4 Democrats make no assumptions about which of these honors the President values most. Each is negotiated separately and matched to a bill as the talks proceed.

How the Deal Works

Four rules govern every item.

  • Nothing takes effect until the law it was traded for takes effect.
  • The more a prize matters to the President, the bigger the bill it is tied to.
  • Every concession is written into a bill, passed by Congress and placed on the public record.
  • Every bill in the plan carries the name Donald J. Trump, so the credit for each accomplishment is unmistakable.

The First 100 Days

Congress convenes on January 3 and introduces all seven priorities together. Democrats open talks with the White House on a first package and publish a public scorecard so voters can follow the progress.

Notes and Sources

Status as of November 11, 2026. Litigation is ongoing, and these facts may change.

  1. Ballroom. U.S. District Judge Richard Leon ordered aboveground work halted on April 16 because Congress had not approved the project, and a divided D.C. Circuit panel upheld that order on August 7. On August 31, the Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that construction could continue while the case proceeds, with the majority indicating the preservationist plaintiffs likely lack standing. The case remains in the lower courts. AP also reported that Congress declined in May to provide the $1 billion the administration requested for the project. Sources: AP via KOB (Aug. 7); Idaho Capital Sun (Aug. 31). Back to text
  2. Arch. The National Capital Planning Commission approved the arch 8-4 on November 5. Within hours, U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan issued a 14-day temporary restraining order blocking construction, and she rejected the administration’s argument that a 1925 statute already authorizes it. Her court has consolidated a suit by veterans and an architectural historian with a separate suit by the District of Columbia and Virginia. Sources: AP via Fox19 (Nov. 6); NPR via WFAE (Nov. 6). Back to text
  3. Kennedy Center. U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper ruled on May 29 that the center cannot be renamed without an act of Congress and ordered Trump’s name removed; the administration has appealed. In August the center’s board voted to restore his name to the facade as part of a longer title crediting him with restoring and renovating the building. Sources: Spokesman-Review (May 29); Daily Maverick (June 12); Newsweek. Back to text
  4. Currency. An 1866 law allows only portraits of deceased people on U.S. currency and securities. Treasury has said commemorative coins fall outside that rule, and the Mint announced on July 15 that it will strike a $1 gold-colored coin with his portrait. Separately, Treasury announced in March that his signature will appear on all future paper currency, starting with the $100 bill. A $250 note with his portrait would require legislation, which has been introduced but has not advanced. Sources: tucson.com; KUTV; Westword (July 15). Back to text
